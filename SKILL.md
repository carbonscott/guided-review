# Guided review

A reviewer's attention is the scarce thing. Reading everything costs them time in
proportion to how much was produced. A guided review costs them time in proportion to how
far the work is from what they meant and from what they think it is.

A review looks back. Its subject is work that exists. The reviewer is the one questioned:
what they meant, what they pictured, whether what they see looks right. Each answer shows a
match or a gap between them and the work. What to do about a gap comes afterwards and is
not asked at a stop.

You do three jobs:

1. **Show** what was made, with just enough context to judge it.
2. **Lead attention** to a few stops. A stop tells them one thing about the work and asks
   them one thing about themselves.
3. **Form the review** from their answers. They answer, you write, they approve.

Their answers are not the review. They are the material for it.

This holds for any work and any maker: code, a design, a document, an analysis; made by a
person, a team or a machine. Nothing here depends on a tool.

## Settle three things first

Take them from what the reviewer already said. Ask only for what is missing, once.

- **The work and what it was for.** Find what was asked for: the request, the goal, the
  brief, the spec, and the reviewer's own words where they exist. If nothing states what
  was wanted, ask.
- **The level.** *Outcome*: does it do what was asked? *Design*: what are the parts and how
  do they fit? *Details*: how does a part work inside? Reviewers of the same work want
  different levels, or several. Never choose for them.
- **The budget.** How much time they will give. A stop takes a minute or two, reading
  included. Ten stops is already a long review.

## Find the gaps yourself

Read the work against what was asked. Verify what needs no judgment: that each claim
matches the evidence it points to, that the numbers agree, that what is said to run does
run. Every place where the work is less than, more than or other than the words of the
request is a finding.

Your checks show whether the work is what it claims to be. They cannot show whether it is
what the reviewer meant. So a finding is something you tell them, and what you ask is
whether it is what they meant.

## Tell first, ask second

You will know more about the work than the reviewer does. Never make them guess what you
already know. A stop has four parts, in this order:

1. **A heading that states the finding as a fact.**
2. **What was asked for, and what is there.** Their own words, quoted, beside a plain
   account of what was made. Numbers, not adjectives.
3. **One figure.** A picture of the made thing, or the one result that carries the point.
   The raw evidence (logs, long output, changed lines) stays folded behind it, with one
   line on where it came from.
4. **One question about them, with bare choices.**

The rules for a stop:

- **It is about something that was made.** Not an open issue, not a next step.
- **The question is one of three.** Is this what you meant? Is this what you pictured? Does
  this look right?
- **The choices are bare.** Yes, partly, no. Looks right, looks wrong. Or two or three
  plain things they might have meant. Never attach a reason to a choice: their reason goes
  in the note. No choice is an action or names a fix.
- **They can answer from what they know and what is shown.** Never ask them to forecast a
  consequence, to schedule, or to choose among remedies.
- **It is one thing and about 70 words of reading.** Several items are several stops.
- **The evidence is real.** Mark anything illustrative as such. If the work has no evidence
  for a point, say so. Never make any.
- **It has honest ways out.** "I cannot tell from this", "not my concern, the maker's
  call", and a free note, on every stop. The first means your stop failed, not them.

**Guess first, rarely.** Let them guess before they see only where their expectation is
itself the finding: a size, a count or a time they have a feel for, on which the request
rested. Never for a fact they could not know; that is a quiz. One or two in a review.

## Few stops, and a list

- **Choose stops by distance.** Take the places where the work sits furthest from the words
  of the request, in the parts that mattered most to them. Do not give each part its turn.
- **Everything else you found goes in a findings list.** One or two sentences each, no
  answer needed, with a mark to ask for a closer look.
- **Keep plans out.** What to fix and when, what to keep or drop, loose ends, open issues:
  none of these is a stop. List them after the findings as decisions that are waiting.
- **Say what you did not look at,** one line each. If nothing needs them, say so plainly.

## Show the thing itself

The overview comes before the stops. For each part: a picture of it or the one number that
sizes it, one or two sentences, whether it is new or changed, and a mark for "look closer"
or "no concern". A reviewer who sees only claims about the work has not seen the work.

## Ways to take input

- **A quick mark.** Yes, partly, no; looks right, looks wrong.
- **Choose among meanings.** Things they might have meant, stated plainly.
- **Guess, then see.** Rarely, as above. The guess locks when the result is shown.
- **Mark the overview or a finding.** "Look closer" or "no concern".
- **A note.** Always offered, never required. Notes often hold what they wanted and never
  said.
- **Go deeper.** Offered on every stop: a further round on that part, one level down.

Show how far along they are. Do not score them.

## The order of a review

What is under review (version and date, what was asked for, the level and budget) → the
overview → the stops → the findings list → what was not looked at → decisions that are
waiting → what you checked (what does not match its evidence, what holds, what you could
not check) → handing the answers back.

One page is the usual form, because it holds pictures beside the questions and takes the
answers there. When nothing needs a picture, asking in plain conversation is enough.

## From answers to the review

They answer where they look, by choosing or marking, never by composing a reply somewhere
else or learning a code. Their answers are saved as they give them, on the machine that
serves the page, and the page says where. They can stop and come back. At most one action
sends them all, and a way to copy the answers as text is always there.

Sending does not wake you. Wait for it if you can, or ask them to say when they have sent.
A review is often left unfinished: an unanswered stop is not reviewed, a guess with no
result seen is only a guess, and silence is never agreement. Keep the answers exactly as
given before you interpret them, and hold each one lightly; some were given quickly.

Then write the review they would have written, short and in their voice:

- an overall verdict: how far the work is from what they meant;
- one line for each gap: what they meant or expected, what is there, and the evidence;
- for each gap, which side is off. If the work is off, that is a request for change. If
  their picture was off, the work may be right, and the finding is for them;
- what they said they want that was never asked for;
- open questions, including every "cannot tell" (give better context, then ask again);
- what was not reviewed.

Next steps come last, as your proposals drawn from the gaps, for them to keep or strike.
Mark every statement that goes beyond what they answered as your reading. Show them the
draft and let them change it. Nothing is sent, posted or acted on until they say so. Run a
further round for each "go deeper" and each "look closer", and fold it into the same
review. If an answer states a lasting preference, ask whether to record it for next time.

## Before you hand it over

Go through it once as the reviewer would: open it, answer every stop, and confirm the
answers come back complete. Then clear your test answers. Count the words of your longest
stop. Check that everything shown is real and that nothing private is on display that they
would not want there.

## What comes with this skill

Two files, as one way to do the above. The directions hold without them.

- `example/index.html` is a wireframe of a review page: every part in its place, each slot
  labelled with what goes there, and no content. Its inputs, saving and copying work. Copy
  it into a new folder with the evidence files beside it and fill the slots. Its first
  lines say what to keep.
- `serve.py` serves that folder from the machine where you work and writes the answers to
  `answers.json` there, with every save kept in `answers.log`. It listens on that machine
  only, so a reviewer elsewhere forwards the port. Give them the address and that one line.
  `python3 serve.py --help` has the rest.

Keep the folder somewhere that lasts. Read the answers from the file once it says
`"done": true`.
