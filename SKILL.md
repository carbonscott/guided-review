# Guided review

Anyone can put the same model on the same work. Two reviews of it still differ, because
the people differ: what each meant, what each knows, and where each looked. The reviewer
is the instrument. Your job is to aim their judgment at the work, spend their attention
where it counts, and record where their judgment reached.

A review looks back at work that exists. What to do about it comes afterwards.

## Principles

1. **The reviewer is the instrument.** Their attention is scarce and their judgment is what
   the review is worth. Spend the first where the second is needed.
2. **Tell what you can check. Ask only what they alone hold.** You can read and verify the
   work. Only they know what they meant, what they pictured, and whether a thing looks
   right to someone who knows the field. Every question asks for one of those three.
3. **Glance at everything, stop at a few, and let them stop anywhere.** Every part is shown
   with a mark to set. The stops follow, the main part first, then by distance from the
   request. Wherever they stop, they hold the best review that time could buy.
4. **Read their grasp from their answers, part by part.** "I cannot tell", "this is news to
   me", a note that says what you did not know: these show where their judgment reaches.
   Never test them for it.
5. **The review says what it covered.** The page records what they answered. The review
   says what that is worth: judged, answered, glanced, could not judge, left to the maker,
   or not reached. Never score the person.
6. **Start with nothing asked.** Find what the work was for yourself. Use what they already
   told you, and let their answers set the depth.

## Before the page

- **Find what was asked for:** the request, goal, brief or spec, and their own words where
  they exist. Ask only if nothing states it, since without it there is no gap to measure.
- **Use what they gave you.** Things they said they want to check become the first stops.
  A level (outcome, design, details) they named holds; otherwise start at the outcome and
  let "go deeper" take it further.
- **Set the budget.** A time they gave holds. If they are there with you, one question
  about time is fine, never required. Otherwise ten minutes. The budget covers everything
  in view, reading and answering. Stated budgets are guesses, so the page shows its own
  cost as they go.
- **Find the gaps yourself.** Read the work against the request. Verify what needs no
  judgment: that claims match their evidence, that numbers agree, that what is said to run
  does run. Every place where the work is less than, more than or other than the request
  is a finding.
- **Divide the work into parts,** the three to seven a reviewer would name. The main part,
  the one the request is mostly about, comes first. Every finding belongs to one part.

## The parts

The page opens with every part. Each shows a picture of it or the one number that sizes it,
one or two sentences, whether it is new or changed, and three marks: *looks right*, *look
closer*, *can't judge this*. The findings of that part sit folded inside it, a sentence or
two each, with a "look closer" tick and no answer needed. A reviewer who sees only claims
about the work has not seen the work. A glance at every part is the review's breadth, and
it should take about two minutes.

## A stop: tell first, ask second

You know more about the work than they do. Never make them guess what you know. A stop has
four parts, in this order:

1. **A heading that states the finding as a fact.**
2. **What was asked for, and what is there.** Their words, quoted, beside a plain account
   of what was made. Numbers, not adjectives.
3. **One figure.** A picture of the made thing, or the one result that carries the point.
   Raw evidence stays folded behind it, with one line on where it came from.
4. **One question about them, with bare choices.**

The rules:

- **It is about something made,** in one part. Not an open issue, not a next step.
- **The question is one of three.** Is this what you meant? Is this what you pictured?
  Does this look right?
- **The choices are bare.** Yes, partly, no. Looks right, looks wrong. Or two or three
  plain things they might have meant. Their reasons go in the note. No choice is an action.
- **They can answer from what they know and what is shown.** Never ask them to forecast,
  to schedule, or to choose a remedy.
- **One thing, about 70 words of reading** in the heading, what was asked for and what is
  there together. Several items are several stops.
- **The evidence is real.** Mark anything illustrative. If there is no evidence, say so.
- **Every stop has the same ways out and signals:** "I knew this" or "news to me", "I
  cannot tell from this", "not my concern", "go deeper", and a note. A "cannot tell" means
  your stop failed, not them.

**Guess first, rarely.** Ask before showing only where their expectation is itself the
finding and hindsight would hide it: a size, a count or a time they had a feel for and the
request rested on. Never a fact they could not know. One or two in a review. The guess
comes first, then the result, then the one question.

**Choose and order the stops.** Their own checks first. Then one on the main part, so the
review can always speak to it. Then the places where the work sits furthest from the
request, in the parts that mattered most to them, and where an approval given without
judgment would cost most. Stop adding when the budget is spent; the rest stay findings.
Do not give each part its turn: the marks already cover every part.

## The rest of the page

After the stops, each of these is one line in view and folded below it:

- **Not looked at:** one line each, with the reason.
- **Decisions that are waiting:** what to fix, keep or drop, loose ends, open issues. They
  are listed and never asked.
- **Checked for you:** what does not match its evidence, what holds, what you could not check.

Then what their answers cover so far, part by part, and the way to send them.

That is the order: what is under review, the parts, the stops, the folded lists, coverage
and hand back. Each step is worth less than the one before, so stopping early loses the
least. When nothing needs a picture, a plain conversation that asks the same things in the
same order is enough.

## Taking the answers

They answer where they look, by choosing or marking, never by writing a reply somewhere
else or learning a code. Answers are saved as they give them, on the machine that serves
the page, and the page says where. They can stop and come back. One action sends them all,
and a way to copy them as text is always there. The page marks how many minutes each
stretch takes and shows, part by part, what they have answered so far. It records; it
does not judge, and it never scores.

Sending does not wake you. Wait for it if you can, or ask them to say when they have sent.
A review is often left unfinished. An unanswered stop is not reviewed, a guess with no
result seen is only a guess, and silence is never agreement. Keep the answers exactly as
given before you interpret them, and hold each lightly: some were given quickly.

## Writing the review

Write the review they would have written, in their voice. The page's rule holds here too:
most needed first. What they would sign fits on one screen, about 300 words:

- **Verdict:** how far the work is from what they meant, and how much of it they judged.
- **Coverage, one line per part.** *Judged* when their answers show judgment: a note, a
  "looks wrong", a choice among meanings, a guess against the result. *Answered* when a
  bare tap is all there is. Then *glanced*, *could not judge*, *left to the maker*, *not
  reached*. A part they could not judge needs better context or another reviewer; say
  which.
- **Gaps, one line each:** what they meant or expected, what is there, and which side is
  off. If the work is off, it is a request for change. If their picture was off, the work
  may be right and the finding is for them. A "yes" on something that was news to them is
  accepted after the fact; say so.
- **What they want that was never asked for,** often found in the notes.

Below that line, for whoever wants more: the evidence for each gap, open questions
(every "cannot tell" with better context, to ask again), next steps as your proposals for
them to keep or strike, and their answers exactly as given.

Mark every statement that goes beyond their answers as your reading. Show them the draft
and let them change it. Nothing is sent, posted or acted on until they say so. Run a
further round for each "go deeper" and "look closer", fold it into the same review, and
update the coverage. If an answer states a lasting preference, ask whether to record it.

## Before you hand it over

Go through the page once as the reviewer would: open it, answer every stop and mark, and
confirm the answers and the coverage come back complete. Then clear your test answers.
Read the time marks: the last stop must end within the budget. Check that everything shown
is real and that nothing private is on display that they would not want there.

## What comes with this skill

Two files, as one way to do the above. The directions hold without them.

- `assets/review-page.html` is a wireframe of a review page: every part in its place, each
  slot labelled with what goes there, and no content. Its marks, folds, time marks,
  saving, copying and the coverage view work. Copy it into a new folder as `index.html`,
  put the evidence files beside it, and fill the slots. Its first lines say what to keep.
- `scripts/serve.py` serves that folder from the machine where you work and writes the
  answers to `answers.json` there, with every save kept in `answers.log`. It listens on
  that machine only, so a reviewer elsewhere forwards the port. Give them the address and
  that one line. `python3 scripts/serve.py --help` has the rest.

Keep the folder somewhere that lasts. Read the answers, coverage included, from the file
once it says `"done": true`.
